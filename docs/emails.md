# Reader & customer email drafts

Writing emails is the most common way a one-person business uses AI, and the easiest place to go wrong. This chapter gives you a repeatable process: AI drafts the messy first version, you make it sound like you, and everybody wins.

## The tone target

Before you touch a prompt, know the voice you want. At Asdale Dynamics we describe it like this:

- **Plain English.** No jargon, no "per my last email," no aspiration to sound like a corporation.
- **Warm but brief.** Friendly doesn't mean long. Two short paragraphs beat one slammed wall of text.
- **Honest.** Never promise what you can't deliver, and never let AI inflate a claim.

## The three-step draft process

### 1. Gather the raw material

Before prompting, collect:

- Who the email is for (one reader, a customer, or your whole list)
- What you actually want to happen (a refund answer? a book sale? a thank-you? a "no"?)
- Any facts that must be stated correctly: dates, prices, order numbers, links

Facts never come from AI. You supply those every single time.

### 2. Prompt like a colleague, not a dictator

Swap "bossy prompt, vague persona" prompts for a pattern like this:

```
Draft a [length, e.g. "under 120 words"] email to [one: a reader / a customer].
Goal: [what should happen after they read it].
Facts to include: [your own facts, verbatim].
Tone: plain English, warm, direct. No emojis. No "I hope this finds you well."
Write it so I only have to tweak a few words.
```

For a one-time situation (a specific complaint, an unusual question), paste a short summary of the situation — **with names and personal details removed.** See [honest limits](honest-limits.html) for why.

### 3. The 60-second pass

Never send an AI draft unread. In about a minute:

1. **Trust check:** Is every fact correct? Dates, amounts, links, names.
2. **Voice check:** Would you say this out loud to a friend? Cut anything you wouldn't say.
3. **Length check:** Delete the first "I hope this finds you well" paragraph if AI wrote one. Delete the second one too.
4. **Promise check:** Does it commit you to anything you can't do this week?

If it does all four checks, send it. If not, edit or redraft with a sharper prompt.

## A prompt library you can grow

Start with these five and add your own as patterns show up:

| Situation | Prompt skeleton |
|---|---|
| Answering a book question | "Reader asked: [summarized question]. Draft a friendly reply answering it using this info: [your facts]." |
| Declining a request | "Polite decline: I can't [request]. Draft a short, kind reply offering [alternative, e.g. the free guide instead]." |
| Following up with a customer | "Draft a two-sentence follow-up about [order/inquiry], friendly, no pressure." |
| Announcing something | "Draft a newsletter blurb about [what/when]. Keep it under 150 words and end with a clear link line." |
| Apologizing | "Draft an apology for [what went wrong]. Sound human, no excuses, state the fix I'm doing: [fix]." |

## The monthly tune-up

Once a month (many people piggyback this on the [money-date](money-date.html)):

- Re-read two real emails you sent. Did AI phrasing slip through? Note the phrases.
- Add one new prompt to your library if a new situation appeared more than twice.
- Delete any template that never felt right. The library should only contain lines you actually use.
