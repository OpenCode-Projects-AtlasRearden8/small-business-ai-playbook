# Honest limits: when NOT to trust AI

This is the most important chapter in the playbook. Everything else assumes you've read it.

AI tools are genuinely useful for a one-person business. They're also confidently wrong in specific, predictable ways. Knowing which is which is the difference between a helpful assistant and an expensive mess.

Here it is straight: **AI doesn't know your business, can't verify facts, and will sound right when it's wrong.**

## The five-line summary

1. **AI can't check facts.** It predicts likely-sounding text, including likely-sounding lies. Every number, date, quote, URL, and legal or tax claim it outputs is unverified until *you* verify it.
2. **AI doesn't know your business.** It knows businesses *like* yours, averaged. Your customers, prices, margins, and history are invisible to it.
3. **AI has no stakes.** You'll still be here when the consequences arrive; it won't, and it doesn't pretend otherwise.
4. **AI biases toward pleasing you.** Its first impulse is agreement. If you ask "is this a good idea?" expect encouragement, not scrutiny — or explicitly ask it to argue the other side.
5. **AI can't feel the relationship.** Tone you approve, contact history you supply, promises you own.

## Do NOT use AI for:

| Situation | Why not | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| Tax filings, tax advice, any filing with penalties | Confident errors are expensive; requirements vary and change | Your ledger, a CPA, official state/IRS sources |
| Legal documents, contracts, registered-agent filings | Plausible ≠ enforceable; jurisdiction-specific | A real attorney; official state forms |
| Medical questions in your health-related content | Confidently fluent medical text is the most convincing wrong text | Never medical advice. Patients need clinicians; your content cites sources, not vibes |
| Anything with account numbers, tax IDs, customer PI | Pasting it anywhere unnecessary adds risk | Strip or replace with placeholders before prompting |
| Laws & regulations you must comply with | Recited confidently, wrong in detail and date | Official sources; the actual statute |
| Deciding money you can't afford to lose | Approval-seeking output + your hope = bad combo | Your ledger, a locked period, a number you can defend |

## Borderline: use with a double-check

- **Numbers of any kind:** AI may *reformat* numbers it was given; it must never be the first place a number exists. Your ledger does arithmetic; AI reads what you wrote afterward.
- **Deadlines and dates:** verify against an actual calendar you own.
- **Research with citations:** ask AI to find sources, then read them yourself. Follow every link it offers; some links are invented.
- **Anything you'd bet your reputation on printing:** read the draft, verifying each claim line by line.

## The AI-approval reflex

Watch for the moment your thought becomes "AI said so, good enough." That's the reflex to interrupt. The playbook's routines are built to keep the "you decide" part real:

- Every routine [here](index.html) ends with **your** three moves, not AI's.
- The [decision journal](decision-journal.html) forbids entries that read "AI suggested..." — reasoning must be yours.
- The [money-date](money-date.html) has AI reviewing *your* analysis, never computing the numbers.

## AI-output flavor errors to watch for

- **Invented quotes and sources.** Real-sounding citations that don't exist.
- **Out-of-date confidence.** AI may not know the current version of a price, plan, or platform feature. Verify what changed recently.
- **Round-number bias.** Suspiciously tidy estimates ("about 20%") are stories, not measurements.
- **One-sided enthusiasm.** "Great question! This will definitely..." — no tool this chatty is neutral.
- **Your own echo.** AI repeats your framing back as if the framing were proven. Give it permission to disagree, then notice it rarely takes full permission without a specific invite.

## A 3-question check before you act on anything AI wrote

1. **Can I verify this in 2 minutes or less with a source I trust?** If yes, verify. If no, don't act on it yet.
2. **Who pays if this is wrong?** If the answer is "you or a customer," it needs verification or a human second opinion.
3. **Would I say this out loud to the person it affects?** If you'd soften or fix it aloud, edit before sending.

## The one-sentence version

**AI drafts, you decide — and when stakes are real, the drafting only even counts after you've checked it line by line.**
